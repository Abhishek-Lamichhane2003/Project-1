Small Binomial Population

Monte Carlo investigation of the sampling distribution of the mean

Author

Abhishek Lamichhane — Student ID: 019102551

Code 
source("R/helpers.R")
set.seed(STUDENT_SEED)

binomial_size <- 5
probability <- 0.10
population_mean <- binomial_size * probability
population_sd <- sqrt(binomial_size * probability * (1 - probability))
generator <- function(sample_size) {
  rbinom(sample_size, size = binomial_size, prob = probability)
}

1 Question and prediction

This population is discrete and strongly concentrated near zero because the probability of success is only 0.10. I expect it to need a larger sample size than a symmetric population before its sample means look smooth and normal.

The theoretical mean is 0.5 and the theoretical standard deviation is 0.6708.

4 Sample sizes investigated

I investigated sample sizes n = 2, 5, 10, 20, 30, 50, 100, and 200. I started with small values to show the discreteness and right skew, included n = 30 because it is a common classroom rule, and used larger values to see how many observations were needed for the sample means to become smoother.

The theoretical standard error is population_sd / sqrt(n). I check whether the simulated standard deviation approaches that value as n increases.

At small sample sizes, the means can remain visibly discrete. With larger sample sizes, the spacing between possible means becomes smaller relative to the overall spread, so the sampling distribution can look more continuous.

6 Approximate normal decision

My final answer is n = 50. At this sample size, the histogram looked smoother and more balanced, and the Q-Q plot was more nearly straight than it was for the smaller sample sizes. The skewness was 0.138, the excess kurtosis was 0.077, and the simulated standard deviation was 0.0937 compared with a theoretical standard error of 0.0949.

The original population is concentrated near zero and is right-skewed. The sample means become smoother as n increases. This value was larger than the die result, which shows that both skewness and discreteness can slow down the normal-looking shape.

7 What surprised me

I was surprised that the Small Binomial distribution needed a larger sample size than the die. Even though both populations are discrete, the low success probability made this population more concentrated and more right-skewed.
